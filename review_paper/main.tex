{\bfseries Abstract}\par\vspace{3pt}
\small
This paper reviews three groundwater studies from the Beas catchment in Himachal Pradesh. Two of
them deal with the Suketi river basin in Mandi district: one describes the groundwater resource and
proposes a development plan, and the other reports the chemistry of groundwater sampled in May and
November 2009. The third study maps groundwater potential in the Gaj watershed of Kangra district
for 2010 and 2022 using GIS and the analytic hierarchy process, and selects sites for recharge
structures. In the Suketi basin, most of the usable groundwater lies in the alluvium of the Balh
valley, where only about 22\% of the assessed resource is in use. The hills depend on small springs,
many of which almost stop flowing before the monsoon. The water is of Ca--HCO\textsubscript{3} type and
is fit for drinking and irrigation, except for high fluoride (4.2~mg/L) in one bore well on granite
and raised nitrate in two towns. In the Gaj watershed, the area of very high potential became smaller
between 2010 and 2022 as monsoon rainfall weakened. The three studies cover quantity, quality and
site selection separately, and none of them links all three. We point out the main gaps in data
and method, and suggest a simple combined approach for future work.
\par\vspace{5pt}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} Groundwater, Springs, Water quality, Groundwater potential, Artificial
recharge, Himachal Himalaya.

\section{Introduction}

Groundwater supplies a large share of the drinking and irrigation water used in India, and
storage has been falling in many northern states \citep{rodell2009}. In the hills of the Himalaya,
the situation is different from the plains. Villages and small towns take their water from
springs, shallow dug wells and hand-pump bore wells. These sources are fed by small aquifers in
weathered and fractured rock, and steep slopes send much of the rain away as runoff. Several
studies have reported falling spring discharge in the region \citep{valdiya1989,niti2018}.

Groundwater in the hills is also poorly measured. In Himachal Pradesh, official resource
estimates are made only for valley areas, so the hill tracts, which cover most of the state, are
not assessed \citep{cgwb2006}. At the same time, demand is rising in the foothill districts because
of population growth, tourism and farming.

Three studies from the Beas catchment address parts of this problem.
\citet{pophare2015} described the groundwater resource of the Suketi river basin and proposed
separate plans for its valley and its hills. \citet{pophare2018} studied the chemistry of
groundwater in the same basin. \citet{sharma2025} mapped groundwater potential in the Gaj
watershed and chose sites for artificial recharge. This review compares the three studies, brings
their main results together and identifies what is still missing. Each paper was read in full,
and its setting, data, methods, results and recommendations were noted. Values that we
calculated from the published data are marked as derived.

The Suketi basin has a central valley, the Balh valley, which holds up to about 370~m of
Quaternary gravel, sand and clay. The valley lies between the Mandi thrust and the Main Central
Thrust, and is surrounded by hills of older meta-sedimentary rocks, granite and limestone
\citep{srikantia1998}. The Gaj watershed is larger, higher and much wetter. Its southern part has
alluvium and Siwalik sediments, while the north and east are made of hard rocks. It is crossed by
the Main Boundary Thrust and several other thrusts \citep{kumar2001}. Both catchments therefore
have one porous aquifer with good storage and a much larger area of fractured hard rock.

The three studies used very different methods. \citet{pophare2015} relied on field work: they
measured water levels in dug wells before and after the monsoon, collected tube-well records,
visited springs and classed them by discharge after \citet{meinzer1923}, and divided the basin into
53 small watersheds for planning. \citet{pophare2018} analysed 14 parameters in each sample
following \citet{apha1998} and judged the results against drinking-water limits and standard
irrigation indices \citep{richards1954}. \citet{sharma2025} built eleven map layers, removed
strongly related layers using the variance inflation factor, gave weights to the remaining layers
with the analytic hierarchy process \citep{saaty1980} and combined them by weighted overlay. They
made four maps (before and after the monsoon in 2010 and in 2022) by changing only the rainfall
layer, and checked the maps with the area under the ROC curve.

\section{Results of the Reviewed Studies}

\subsection{Where the Groundwater Is}

\subsubsection{Suketi valley}
In the Balh valley, dug wells up to about 15~m deep tap an unconfined aquifer, and tube wells,
mostly 50--125~m deep, tap layers of sand and gravel below clay \citep{pophare2015}. Some tube
wells flow on their own at the surface. Most tube wells yield 9--45~\lps{}. In 2009 the water
table was within 12~m of the surface before the monsoon and within 10~m after it, and 27 of the 28
observation wells changed by less than 2~m between seasons. In more than half of the wells the
water stood within 4~m of the surface.

The net annual groundwater available in the valley was estimated at 3.48~\Mm{}, while use was
only 0.78~\Mm{}, so the stage of development was about 22\% \citep{cgwb2006}. The valley aquifer is
therefore under-used and could supply more water if pumping is planned with care.

\subsubsection{Suketi hills}
The hills are much poorer in groundwater. Most of the 93 springs studied are fracture or
depression springs. In May 2009 many were dry or gave less than 0.01~\lps{}, and in November most
gave only 0.1--0.4~\lps{}. On Meinzer's scale, most belong to the three smallest classes (VI to
VIII). Only a few springs linked to limestone or major faults, near Nalni, Nagchalla and Mandi,
gave more than 10~\lps{}. The drainage network is dense and fine, which means that rain leaves the
slopes quickly \citep{pophare2014}. The authors identified three slope belts: hills steeper than
10\textdegree{} that mostly shed water, a foot-hill belt of 5--10\textdegree{} that is the main
recharge zone, and the flat valley floor where groundwater comes out.

\subsubsection{Gaj watershed}
In the Gaj watershed, zones of moderate to very high potential follow the main thrusts and
lineaments and the southern alluvium, while the steep northern slopes score low
\citep{sharma2025}. Fig.~\ref{fig:gwp} shows the share of the area in each class. The
very high class fell from 4.20\% to 2.63\% of the area before the monsoon and from 4.46\% to 3.20\%
after it. The maps scored AUC values of 0.82 and 0.81, which is usually read as good accuracy.

The two catchments point to the same controls even though the methods differ. Faults, thrusts and
lineaments guide both spring locations and high-potential zones. Slope decides whether rain soaks
in or runs off. Valley fill and alluvium are the only aquifers with large storage.

\subsection{Water Quality}

Only the Suketi basin has chemical data \citep{pophare2018}. The main results are listed in
Table~\ref{tab:quality}. All waters are slightly alkaline and have low salt content. Conductivity
was highest in dug wells (582~\uScm{} in May) and lowest in springs (350~\uScm{}), and every source
was more dilute after the monsoon. Calcium and bicarbonate are the main ions, so most samples
plot as Ca--\ce{HCO3} water on the Piper diagram \citep{piper1944}. On the Gibbs diagram most
samples fall in the rainfall-dominated field \citep{gibbs1970}. This suggests that water passes
through the steep terrain quickly and has little time to react with the rock. Spring water and
river water have almost the same ionic make-up, which indicates that hill springs feed the base
flow of the Suketi river.

Two local problems were found. A bore well at Pabu, on granite, had 4.2~\mgl{} fluoride in May and
2.2~\mgl{} in November, above the limit of 1.5~\mgl{} \citep{bis2012,who2017}. The authors linked
this to fluoride released from the granite. Nitrate reached 36--37~\mgl{} in a spring at Mandi and
28--32~\mgl{} in a bore well at Rewalsar. These values are below the 45~\mgl{} limit, but both sites
are in crowded towns, and nitrate is likely to come from sewage and farm runoff. For irrigation,
all samples were safe on the sodium and carbonate indices. Six samples in each season were
corrosive, which matters for metal pipes in supply schemes.

\subsection{Change over Time}

The Suketi data come from one year (2009), so they show only the seasonal pattern. The Gaj study
covers 2010 to 2022. Over this period, monsoon rainfall in the watershed was lower in several
years, and the area of very high potential decreased \citep{sharma2025}. Government records quoted
in that study show water-level falls of up to 7.16~m in the area, and 235 of the 4,535 hand pumps
in the area needed repair or replacement, partly because of low water levels. Maximum temperature
rose over the same period, and the population of Kangra district grew by about 12\% between 2011
and 2023 (derived).

These trends should be read with care. Rainfall was the only layer that changed between the 2010
and 2022 maps, so the change in the maps reflects changed rainfall and not a measured change in
groundwater storage.

\subsection{Recharge and Management Proposals}

\citet{pophare2015} suggested separate plans for the valley and the hills. In the valley,
well-designed dug wells can meet most local demand, recharge should be tried where post-monsoon
water levels are deeper than about 3~m, and old village ponds should be cleaned and deepened. In the
hills, they proposed about three check dams or gabions for each spring, roughly 282 structures in
all, together with rooftop rainwater harvesting and farm ponds. Structures were matched to stream
order, from gully plugs on small streams to check dams on larger ones \citep{cgwb2007}. They also
asked for the revival of traditional systems such as \textit{baudis}, \textit{nauns} and
\textit{kuhls} \citep{sharma2009}.

\citet{sharma2025} placed candidate sites where lineaments cross streams and kept only those on
gentle or moderate slopes and suitable land. Priority was given to crowded foothill areas. They
recommended check dams, gabions, contour trenches, pony-pit wells and roof-water tanks, and
avoided steep slopes because of fast runoff and the risk of slope failure.

Both studies favour small, low-cost structures on small streams and gentle ground. Neither
estimates how much water the structures would add, what they would cost, or who would maintain
them. These points often decide whether recharge schemes work in practice \citep{bouwer2002}.

\section{Discussion}

The three studies are useful together because each covers a different question. The first shows
where water is stored and how much is used. The second shows that the water is mostly safe and
that hill springs feed the rivers. The third covers the whole watershed in a consistent way and
looks at change over twelve years. Table~\ref{tab:appraisal} lists the main strengths and
weaknesses.

Three weaknesses are shared or especially important. First, the Suketi data are from a single
year and are now old. Second, the Gaj maps have no direct field check. The authors compared them
with an infiltration map, but that map was built from five layers that were also used in the
potential model, so close agreement is partly expected. Testing against spring flow or well
levels would be stronger \citep{machiwal2011}. Third, no study looks at water quality and recharge
together. The Suketi results show fluoride in granite and nitrate in towns. The Gaj study proposes
recharge in crowded foothills and also has granite in its catchment, so recharge water there
should be tested before structures are built.

The methods could also be shared between the two catchments. A spring and well inventory like the
one in the Suketi basin would give the Gaj maps the field data they lack. In turn, the mapping
method of the Gaj study could be used in the Suketi hills, where no official estimates exist, to
decide which of the 53 small watersheds should get structures first.

Fig.~\ref{fig:framework} shows a simple combined approach. Field data, chemistry and satellite data
are analysed separately and then merged into one recharge map that excludes polluted or
unsuitable sites. Sites are planned at the scale of single spring catchments, as recommended for
the Himalaya \citep{niti2018,tambe2012}, and a monitoring network feeds the results back into the
next round of planning.

\section{Future Work}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Set up observation wells and gauged springs and measure them every season.
  \item Use stable isotopes and geological mapping to find the recharge areas of important springs.
  \item Extend water sampling to the Gaj watershed, and add trace metals and bacteria.
  \item Test how sensitive the potential maps are to the chosen weights, and check them against field data.
  \item Estimate how much water each recharge structure adds, and record its cost and upkeep.
  \item Involve local communities, and restore traditional water systems along with new structures.
\end{enumerate}

% =====================================================================
\section{Conclusions}

The studies reviewed here show that groundwater in these Himalayan catchments occurs in two very
different settings. The valley fill of the Balh valley holds a large and little-used resource with
shallow water levels. The hills depend on small springs that weaken before every monsoon. The
water of the Suketi basin is generally good for drinking and irrigation, but fluoride in granite
areas and nitrate in towns need regular checks. In the Gaj watershed, the zones most favourable for
groundwater shrank between 2010 and 2022 as rainfall declined.

Each study covers one part of the problem: quantity, quality or site selection. Future work should
combine them, test maps against field data, screen recharge sites for water quality and plan at the
scale of spring catchments. This would give local planners a sounder basis for managing
groundwater in the Himalayan foothills.
