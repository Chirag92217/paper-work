{\bfseries Abstract}\par\vspace{3pt}
\small
Mountain aquifers of the Indian Himalaya sustain drinking water, minor irrigation and
spring flow for a large and growing population, yet they remain far less understood than
the aquifers of the plains. This review compares three studies from two Lesser Himalayan
catchments of Himachal Pradesh: a hydrogeological appraisal of groundwater development in
the Suketi river basin (Mandi district), a two-season hydrochemical survey of the same
basin, and a geospatial multi-criteria analysis of groundwater potential and recharge
siting in the Gaj watershed (Kangra district). The studies are read through a common
framework covering physical setting, data and methods, groundwater occurrence, water
quality, temporal behaviour and management proposals. In the Suketi basin, usable
groundwater is concentrated in the valley-fill of the Balh valley, where only about 22\% of
the assessed dynamic resource is drawn, while the surrounding hills rely on low-yield
fracture and depression springs that mostly fall in Meinzer magnitudes VI to VIII. The
groundwater there is a slightly alkaline Ca--HCO\textsubscript{3} water that is generally fit for
drinking and irrigation, apart from one granite-hosted bore well with 4.2~mg/L fluoride and
raised nitrate near the towns of Mandi and Rewalsar. In the Gaj watershed, the share of very
high groundwater potential fell between 2010 and 2022 together with weaker monsoon
rainfall, and the most promising recharge sites lie on gentle to moderate slopes in the
populated foothills. Taken together, the studies move from quantity to quality to spatial
planning, but none of them joins all three. The main gaps are single-year chemical data, a
lack of isotopic and observation-well records, partial circularity in map validation and
little socio-economic appraisal. An integrated quantity--quality--space framework is
proposed to guide springshed-based recharge planning in the region.

\section{Introduction}

India draws more groundwater than any other country, and a large part of its drinking
water and irrigation supply now depends on wells rather than on surface storage. Satellite
gravity records have shown sustained loss of groundwater storage across northern India
\citep{rodell2009}, and global budgeting suggests that the abstraction from several major
aquifers already exceeds what natural recharge can replace \citep{gleeson2012}. The
National Water Policy therefore asks that groundwater be planned at the scale of the
hydrological unit, with quantity, quality and environmental needs considered together
\citep{mowr2002}.

The Himalaya is often called the water tower of Asia, because its snow, ice and monsoon
rain feed the great river systems downstream \citep{immerzeel2010}. Within the mountains,
however, settlements in the middle hills seldom use those large rivers directly. They rely
on springs, shallow dug wells, hand-pump bore wells and a range of traditional structures
that tap small, fractured and strongly compartmentalised aquifers. Reports of falling spring
discharge go back more than three decades in the Kumaun Himalaya \citep{valdiya1989}, and a
national working group estimated that a large share of Himalayan springs are drying or
becoming seasonal \citep{niti2018}. Field experiments in Sikkim have shown that targeted
recharge in the spring catchment can restore flow \citep{tambe2012}, but such work needs
basic knowledge of where water enters the ground, how it moves along structures, and how
good it is once it emerges.

Hard-rock hill tracts are also difficult to assess with the standard methods used on the
plains. Steep slopes push most rainfall into fast runoff, thrusts and fractures make
aquifers patchy, and the observation network is thin. In Himachal Pradesh, official
groundwater resources are computed only for the valley areas; the hills, which make up most
of the land, are not quantified \citep{cgwb2006,pophare2015}. Meanwhile, population, tourism
and irrigated farming are expanding in the foothill districts of Mandi and Kangra.

Against this background, three studies from the Beas catchment offer an instructive
contrast. \citet{pophare2015} described the groundwater regime of the Suketi river basin and
proposed separate development plans for its central valley and surrounding hills.
\citet{pophare2018} examined the chemistry of groundwater and river water in the same basin
across the pre- and post-monsoon seasons of 2009. \citet{sharma2025} used remote sensing, GIS
and the analytic hierarchy process (AHP) to map groundwater potential in the Gaj watershed
for 2010 and 2022 and to select sites for artificial recharge. The three works therefore
apply field hydrogeology, hydrochemistry and geospatial decision analysis to neighbouring
Lesser and Outer Himalayan catchments, and together they span data collected between 2009
and 2022.

No synthesis of these studies is available. The present review aims to: (i) compare their
settings, data and methods; (ii) bring together their findings on groundwater occurrence,
quality, temporal change and recharge; (iii) evaluate the strengths and weaknesses of each
approach; and (iv) identify research gaps and outline an integrated framework for
groundwater management in similar Himalayan watersheds.

\section{Approach of the Review}

The review follows a structured comparative design (Fig.~\ref{fig:workflow}). The three
studies were chosen because they share a regional setting (tributary catchments of the Beas
river in Himachal Pradesh), address complementary parts of the groundwater problem, and
report their data in enough detail to allow quantitative comparison. Each paper was read in
full, and information was extracted under six themes: physical setting; data sources and
sampling design; analytical methods; groundwater occurrence and potential; water quality;
and management or recharge proposals. Numerical results were taken from the text and tables
of the source papers. Where the present review derives additional indicators, such as
percentage changes between years or shares of wells in a depth class, these are stated as
derived values. The extracted material was then examined for agreement, contrast and
missing elements, which form the basis of the discussion and of the framework proposed
at the end.

\section{Physical Setting of the Study Areas}

\subsection{Suketi River Basin}

The Suketi river joins the Beas near Mandi and drains an elongated basin of about 422~\kmsq{}
between Mandi in the north and Sundernagar in the south (31\textdegree27$'$--31\textdegree45$'$N;
76\textdegree48$'$--77\textdegree03$'$E). Its most distinctive feature is the Balh valley, an
intermontane basin of about 79~\kmsq{} (roughly 19\% of the catchment) lying near 790~m above
mean sea level. The valley is filled with up to about 370~m of Quaternary boulders, gravel,
sand, silt and clay, and it is bounded by the Mandi thrust and the Main Central Thrust
\citep{srikantia1998,pophare2018}. The surrounding hills cover about 343~\kmsq{} and rise from
800~m to nearly 2,900~m. They expose the Dhauladhar granite, Proterozoic meta-sediments of the
Shali, Sundernagar and Salkhala groups, and Cenozoic rocks of the Subathu and Siwalik groups.

Mandi district receives about 1,136~mm of rain in an average year, mostly between July and
September, and the higher peaks receive winter snow \citep{cgwb2006}. Morphometric analysis
classed the basin as seventh order, with 1,671 stream segments, a drainage density of
2.69~km/km\textsuperscript{2}, a basin relief of 2,130~m and a ruggedness number of 5.73
\citep{pophare2014,pophare2015}. Its elongation ratio (0.80) and form factor (0.50) point to an
elongated shape that delays peak runoff, which the authors regarded as a modest advantage for
recharge.

\subsection{Gaj Watershed}

The Gaj watershed lies in the Kangra valley, roughly between 32\textdegree00$'$--32\textdegree25$'$N
and 76\textdegree00$'$--76\textdegree25$'$E, and drains about 900~\kmsq{} towards the Beas
\citep{sharma2025}. Its upper reaches climb to about 4,500~m along the Dhauladhar range, and it
receives far more precipitation than the Suketi basin, around 2,600~mm a year, with heavy
monsoon rain on the southern slopes and snow at higher levels. Porous formations (recent
alluvium in the south, and the Pinjore and Dhok Pathan units of the Siwalik group in the west)
contrast with fissured hard rocks: the eastern Chail formation and, towards the north, the
Dhauladhar granite. The area is crossed by the Main Boundary Thrust and by the Jawalamukhi, Drini,
Panjal and Chail thrusts, and most springs follow these structures \citep{kumar2001,sharma2025}.

\subsection{Comparison of Settings}

Table~\ref{tab:setting} summarises the two settings. The Gaj watershed is about twice as large,
reaches a much higher elevation and receives more than twice the rainfall of the Suketi basin.
Both, however, combine a productive unconsolidated aquifer (the Balh valley fill and the
southern Kangra alluvium) with large tracts of fractured hard rock where groundwater is
stored in weathered zones, joints and faults. This shared two-part structure is the key to
comparing the studies.

\section{Comparison of Methods}

\subsection{Field Hydrogeological Appraisal}

\citet{pophare2015} built their account on a 1:50,000 base map prepared from Survey of India
toposheets. In the valley, 28 dug wells were set up as key observation wells and measured in
May and November 2009; the depth to water level was contoured, and records of 64 tube wells
were collected from the Irrigation and Public Health Department. In the hills, 93 springs were
visited and described by type, host rock and discharge, and their size was rated on the
classification of \citet{meinzer1923}. Thematic maps of geology, drainage, soils, land use,
lineaments and slope were prepared, and stream ordering followed \citet{strahler1957}. The
basin was then divided into 53 micro-watersheds within five sub-basins to serve as planning
units. Harvestable runoff was estimated using three empirical approaches (Strange's
coefficients, Binnie's percentages and the Inglis formula), and the lowest result was retained
for recharge planning. Resource figures for the valley were taken from official estimates
\citep{cgwb2006}.

\subsection{Hydrochemical Survey}

\citet{pophare2018} collected 31 groundwater samples in each of the two seasons of 2009: five
from dug wells and six from tube wells in the valley, and thirteen from springs and seven from
bore wells in the hills. Five river samples were added for comparison. pH, electrical
conductivity (EC) and total dissolved solids (TDS) were recorded on site, and the major
ions, hardness, nitrate and fluoride were determined following standard methods
\citep{apha1998}. The results were interpreted with a correlation matrix, the classification of
\citet{schoeller1967}, the trilinear diagram of \citet{piper1944}, chloro-alkaline indices and
the diagram of \citet{gibbs1970}. Suitability for drinking was judged against national and
international limits; suitability for irrigation was judged with a set of indices
(Eqs.~\ref{eq:sar}--\ref{eq:pi}, all concentrations in meq/L) and the salinity diagram of
\citet{richards1954}:
\begin{align}
\mathrm{SAR} &= \frac{\mathrm{Na}}{\sqrt{(\mathrm{Ca}+\mathrm{Mg})/2}} \label{eq:sar}\\[2pt]
\%\mathrm{Na} &= \frac{\mathrm{Na}+\mathrm{K}}{\mathrm{Ca}+\mathrm{Mg}+\mathrm{Na}+\mathrm{K}}\times 100 \label{eq:pna}\\[2pt]
\mathrm{RSC} &= (\mathrm{HCO_3}+\mathrm{CO_3})-(\mathrm{Ca}+\mathrm{Mg}) \label{eq:rsc}\\[2pt]
\mathrm{KR} &= \frac{\mathrm{Na}}{\mathrm{Ca}+\mathrm{Mg}} \label{eq:kr}\\[2pt]
\mathrm{PI} &= \frac{\mathrm{Na}+\sqrt{\mathrm{HCO_3}}}{\mathrm{Ca}+\mathrm{Mg}+\mathrm{Na}}\times 100 \label{eq:pi}
\end{align}
Soluble sodium percentage, magnesium ratio and corrosivity ratio were also computed, together
with the Collins ratio as a drinking-water indicator.

\subsection{Geospatial Multi-criteria Analysis}

\citet{sharma2025} prepared eleven factor maps (aspect, curvature, drainage density, geology,
lineament density, land use/land cover, rainfall, soil, slope, stream order and temperature)
from a 12.5~m ALOS PALSAR elevation model, LISS-IV imagery, FAO soil data and Indian
Meteorological Department records. The land-use map reached an overall accuracy of 86.3\% and a
kappa coefficient of 0.84. Collinearity among predictors was checked with the variance
inflation factor (VIF),
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{VIF}_i=\frac{1}{1-R_i^{2}},
\label{eq:vif}
\end{equation}
where $R_i^2$ is obtained by regressing factor $i$ on the others. All retained layers had VIF
values between 1.88 and 3.65, below the commonly used limit of 5, although such cut-offs should
be applied with care \citep{obrien2007}. Layer and class weights were then derived by pairwise
comparison \citep{saaty1980}, with the consistency of each matrix checked through
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{CR}=\frac{\mathrm{CI}}{\mathrm{RI}},\qquad \mathrm{CI}=\frac{\lambda_{\max}-n}{n-1},
\label{eq:cr}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda_{\max}$ is the principal eigenvalue, $n$ the number of criteria and RI the random
index; matrices were accepted when CR was below 0.1. The groundwater potential index of each
cell was obtained by weighted overlay,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{GWPI}_i=\sum_{j=1}^{n} w_j\, r_{ij},
\label{eq:gwpi}
\end{equation}
where $w_j$ is the weight of factor $j$ and $r_{ij}$ the rating of its class at cell $i$
\citep{malczewski2006}. Four maps were produced (pre- and post-monsoon for 2010 and 2022) by
changing only the rainfall layer, and each was split into five classes using natural breaks.
Accuracy was tested with the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve
\citep{fawcett2006}. A separate infiltration-capacity index was built from soil, slope,
temperature, land use and curvature (CR = 0.072) and cross-tabulated with the potential maps.
Candidate recharge points were placed at intersections of lineaments with the drainage network
and filtered by slope, land use and groundwater potential.

\subsection{Methodological Contrasts}

The three approaches sit at different points on a scale from direct observation to inference.
The Suketi studies are grounded in measurements at wells and springs but extend those
measurements across the basin only qualitatively. The Gaj study covers every cell of the
watershed in a consistent way but depends on expert weights and on proxies for the subsurface.
Field inventories capture what is actually happening at a source; index models capture where
conditions favour storage and recharge. Neither is sufficient alone, and the two are best seen
as partners: point data can calibrate and test the maps, and the maps can guide where new
points should be measured.

\section{Synthesis of Findings}

\subsection{Groundwater Occurrence and Resource Potential}

\subsubsection{Valley aquifers}
In the Balh valley, dug wells no deeper than about 15~m tap a phreatic aquifer, while tube
wells, mostly 50--125~m deep (recorded range about 27--135~m), draw from layered sand, gravel and boulder horizons separated from
the shallow zone by clay \citep{pophare2015}. Some tube wells flow freely at the surface,
showing confined conditions. Reported yields of tube wells range from about 5.5 to 84~\lps{},
with most between 9 and 45~\lps{}. In 2009 the water table stood within 12~m of the surface
before the monsoon and within 10~m after it, and 27 of the 28 observation wells (96\%) changed by
less than 2~m between seasons (Table~\ref{tab:valley}). By derived count, about 54\% of wells in
May and 61\% in November had water levels shallower than 4~m, and 21--36\% were shallower than
2~m, which indicates a tendency to waterlogging in parts of the valley.

According to official estimates as on March 2004, the net annual groundwater availability of
the valley was 3.48~\Mm{} against an annual draft of 0.78~\Mm{}, giving a stage of development
of about 22\% \citep{cgwb2006,pophare2015}. A stage this low means that the valley aquifer is
under-used rather than stressed, and it could support further, carefully planned abstraction.

\subsubsection{Hill aquifers and springs}
The hills of the Suketi basin present a very different picture. Groundwater is held in thin
weathered mantles and in fractures, and it reaches people through springs and through bore
wells of about 50--70~m depth fitted with hand pumps \citep{pophare2015}. Most of the 93 springs
inventoried are fracture or depression springs in sandstone, phyllite or boulder beds. In May
2009 many of them were dry or produced less than 0.01~\lps{}, and in November most yielded only
about 0.1--0.4~\lps{}; on Meinzer's scale the majority fall in magnitudes VI to VIII. A handful of
springs linked to limestone solution features or major structures, such as those near Nalni,
Nagchalla and Mandi, flow at more than 10~\lps{}. The authors also noted that many fracture
springs revive only briefly after the monsoon, which points to small storage and fast drainage.

Morphometry supports this reading. The very fine drainage texture (14.88) and high
infiltration number (10.66) indicate a landscape that sheds water quickly
\citep{pophare2014}. Slope zoning separates three belts: hills steeper than 10\textdegree{} that
act mainly as runoff zones, a piedmont belt of 5--10\textdegree{} that serves as the main recharge
zone, and the valley floor below 5\textdegree{} where groundwater discharges \citep{pophare2015}.

\subsubsection{Groundwater potential in the Gaj watershed}
In the Gaj watershed, moderate to very high potential is concentrated along the major thrusts
and lineaments and over the southern alluvium, while steep northern slopes score low
\citep{sharma2025}. Table~\ref{tab:gwp} and Fig.~\ref{fig:gwp} give the class areas. The very
high class shrank from 4.20\% to 2.63\% of the area in the pre-monsoon maps and from 4.46\% to
3.20\% in the post-monsoon maps between 2010 and 2022, a relative decline of about 37\% and 28\%,
respectively (derived). The high class changed much less. In the post-monsoon maps, the low and

distribution became more concentrated in the middle classes. The authors reported AUC values of
0.82 and 0.81, and found more than 90\% agreement between the high classes of the potential and
infiltration maps.

\subsubsection{Common controls}
Although the methods differ, both catchments point to the same controls. Structures (thrusts,
faults and lineaments) concentrate both springs and high potential; slope decides whether rain
runs off or infiltrates; and unconsolidated valley fill or alluvium forms the only aquifer with
appreciable storage. The Suketi work reaches these conclusions from field observation, while
the Gaj work expresses them as factor weights. The agreement between two independent lines of
evidence gives confidence that these controls are general for the Lesser and Outer Himalaya of
the region.

\subsection{Groundwater Quality}

Water quality was examined only in the Suketi basin \citep{pophare2018}. The key outcomes are
summarised in Table~\ref{tab:quality}.

\subsubsection{Mineralisation and facies}
All waters are slightly alkaline, with mean pH between about 7.3 and 7.9, and their
mineral content is low. Mean EC follows the order dug wells $>$ bore wells $>$ tube wells $>$ river
water $>$ springs in both seasons, and every source is more dilute after the monsoon
(Fig.~\ref{fig:ec}). Bicarbonate is the dominant anion and calcium the dominant cation, followed
by magnesium and sodium; total hardness stays below 300~\mgl{} and is mainly of the carbonate
(temporary) kind. On the Piper diagram most samples plot as Ca--\ce{HCO3} water, in which alkaline
earths exceed alkalis and weak acids exceed strong acids. Strong correlations of calcium and
magnesium with bicarbonate point to carbonate and silicate weathering as a shared source
\citep{hem1985}.

\subsubsection{Controlling processes}
On the Gibbs diagram most samples fall in the field of precipitation dominance and only a few
show the influence of rock weathering. Combined with the low TDS, this suggests that recharge
water moves quickly through the steep terrain and has little time to react with the rocks
\citep{pophare2018}. The chloro-alkaline indices were positive in about 68\% of samples before
the monsoon but in only 45\% after it, implying that fresh recharge temporarily shifts the
balance of ion exchange. A further useful result is that spring water and river water have
nearly identical ionic make-up, supporting the view that shallow hill aquifers sustain the
base flow of the Suketi and its tributaries.

\subsubsection{Contamination signals}
Two local problems stand out. A bore well at Pabu, on granite, contained 4.2~\mgl{} fluoride in
May and 2.2~\mgl{} in November, well above the 1.5~\mgl{} limit \citep{bis2012,who2017}; the
authors attribute this to release from fluoride-bearing minerals during long contact with the
host rock. Second, nitrate reached 36--37~\mgl{} in a spring at Mandi and 28--32~\mgl{} in a bore
well at Rewalsar, far above the values found at most other sites. These levels are
below the 45~\mgl{} limit, but their location in crowded towns and their correlation with
sulphate and potassium point to sewage and farm inputs. Such early signals deserve
regular monitoring because nitrate tends to rise as settlements grow.

\subsubsection{Suitability for irrigation}
All irrigation indices fall in safe ranges (Table~\ref{tab:quality}). SAR is below 10 in every
sample, and most samples plot in the C2--S1 class (medium salinity, low sodium hazard) of the
salinity diagram \citep{richards1954}. Residual sodium carbonate is below 1.25~meq/L, Kelley's
ratio is below 1, and the permeability index averages 58--67\%, which falls within the suitable
classes of \citet{doneen1964}. The magnesium ratio exceeded 50 in nine samples before the
monsoon but in only one afterwards. The main practical concern is the corrosivity ratio, which
was above 1 in six samples in each season and could shorten the life of metal pipes in water
supply schemes.

\subsubsection{Relevance to the Gaj watershed}
No chemical data are reported for the Gaj watershed. Yet the two areas share the same kinds
of rock, including the Dhauladhar granite, and the same pressures from growing towns. The
Suketi results therefore suggest that fluoride should be checked in granite-hosted wells and
springs of the Gaj, and that nitrate should be watched in the foothill settlements that
\citet{sharma2025} identified as priorities for recharge. This matters because a recharge
structure built where runoff collects from roads, drains or farm land can carry pollutants
into the very aquifer it is meant to protect.

\subsection{Temporal Behaviour and Climate Signal}

The Suketi studies capture a single year and show only seasonal behaviour: modest water-level
rise in the valley and strong post-monsoon dilution of the water. The Gaj study adds a decadal
view. Monsoon rainfall in the watershed was markedly lower in several years after 2010, and the
potential maps for 2022 show a smaller very high class \citep{sharma2025}. Official records cited
in that study report water-table rises of up to 2.94~m and falls of up to 7.16~m in the area, a
decadal water-level variation of about 6~m in Kangra, and 235 of 4,535 hand pumps in need of
repair or abandonment, partly because of low water levels. Maximum temperature increased and
minimum temperature decreased between 2010 and 2023, which may lengthen dry spells and raise
evaporative demand. At the same time, the population of Kangra district grew from about 1.51
million in 2011 to about 1.69 million in 2023, an increase of nearly 12\% (derived).

One caution applies. Because rainfall was the only layer that changed between the 2010 and
2022 maps, the shift in potential classes reflects changed recharge conditions rather than a
measured change in stored groundwater. The trend agrees with the official water-level records,
which is encouraging, but it should not be read as a direct measurement of depletion.

\subsection{Management and Recharge Proposals}

Table~\ref{tab:manage} compares the management proposals. Both studies favour small, low-cost
structures placed on low-order streams and gentle slopes, but they reach their recommendations
in different ways.

\citet{pophare2015} argued that the valley and the hills need separate plans. For the valley,
the low stage of development and shallow water levels mean that domestic and irrigation demand
can be met from adequately sized dug wells; managed recharge is recommended where
post-monsoon levels are deeper than about 3~m, and old village ponds could be desilted and
deepened to store more water. For the hills, the authors recommend about three check dams or
gabion structures (each roughly 15~m wide and 1.5~m high) for every spring, or around 282
structures for the 94 springs counted in their plan, along with rooftop rainwater harvesting, farm ponds and
small underground tanks. They also match structures to stream order: gully plugs and gabions on
first- and second-order streams, nala bunds and percolation tanks on second- and third-order
streams, and check dams or subsurface dykes on fourth- to seventh-order channels
\citep{cgwb2007}. Harvestable runoff of the basin was estimated at 262--375~\Mm{} a year, which is
about 75 times the assessed groundwater availability of the valley (derived), so water
for recharge is not the limiting factor; the limiting factors are storage and suitable sites.
Finally, they stress the revival of traditional systems such as \textit{baudis} (stone-lined
spring pits), \textit{nauns} (covered spring tanks), \textit{chhrudus} (piped spring outlets) and
\textit{kuhls} (gravity irrigation channels), many of which are falling into disuse
\citep{sharma2009}.

\citet{sharma2025} used a spatial filter instead of a rule of thumb. Candidate points at
lineament--drainage intersections were kept only on low to moderate slopes and on suitable land
uses, and were then ranked by the needs of populated foothill areas. The suggested structures
include check dams, gabions, contour trenches, pony-pit wells, roof-water storage tanks and
water-spreading weirs.
Steep sites were rejected both because water runs off too quickly and because heavy structures
might destabilise slopes, a practical point for a seismically active belt.

Neither study estimates how much recharge the proposed structures would add, what they would
cost, or who would own and maintain them. Experience with managed recharge shows that these
questions largely decide whether a scheme succeeds \citep{bouwer2002}.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{How the Studies Complement Each Other}

Read in order of publication, the three studies show a clear shift in method over a decade:
from field-based descriptive hydrogeology, through process-oriented hydrochemistry, to
data-driven spatial decision support. Each answers a question the others leave open. The
resource study shows where water is stored and how it is used, but it cannot say whether the
water is safe or how conditions vary across unsampled hills. The chemical study shows that the
water is generally safe and that hill aquifers feed the rivers, but it says little about volume.
The geospatial study covers the whole watershed and tracks change through time, but it has no
direct measure of water levels or quality. A combined reading is therefore more useful for
planning than any single paper.

\subsection{Strengths and Limitations}

Table~\ref{tab:appraisal} summarises the main strengths and weaknesses. Several points need
emphasis.

\textit{Temporal coverage.} Both Suketi data sets come from a single hydrological year (2009).
Seasonal contrasts are captured, but year-to-year variability and long-term trends are not, and
the data are now more than fifteen years old. The resource figures refer to 2004.

\textit{Observation data.} In the Gaj study, the absence of observation-well records is
acknowledged by the authors themselves. Without measured water levels or spring discharge, the
potential maps remain hypotheses about where groundwater should be, rather than evidence of
where it is.

\textit{Subjectivity and validation.} AHP weights rest on expert judgement, and no sensitivity
analysis was reported to show how the maps would change under different weights. The
description of which layers entered the final overlay is also not fully consistent between
sections of the paper. More importantly, the infiltration map used to cross-check the potential
maps was built from five layers (soil, slope, temperature, land use and curvature) that also
enter the potential model. A high level of agreement between the two maps is therefore partly
expected and is not fully independent validation. Testing against measured spring discharge,
well yields or water-level change would be much stronger \citep{machiwal2011,fawcett2006}.

\textit{Analytical scope.} The chemical survey covers the major ions, nitrate and fluoride, but
not trace metals, microbial quality or stable isotopes. Bacterial contamination of springs is a
common health problem in hill towns, and isotopes of oxygen and hydrogen could identify the
altitude and location of spring recharge areas, which is exactly the information needed to
place recharge structures \citep{niti2018}. The drinking-water limits used in 2018 were also
earlier editions of the current standards \citep{bis2012}.

\textit{Resource accounting.} In the Suketi study, the hills were not quantified, and the
recommended number of structures follows a uniform rule of three per spring rather than a water
balance for each spring catchment. The runoff estimate itself varies by about 40\% between
methods, and the rainfall used for it (1,350~mm) differs from the district average cited
elsewhere (1,136~mm).

\textit{Social dimension.} Both studies recognise the importance of local communities, but
neither assesses land ownership, willingness to maintain structures, or costs and benefits.
The Suketi study does, however, offer a rare record of traditional water systems, which could
anchor community-based management \citep{sharma2009}.

\subsection{Transferability Between the Two Catchments}

Because the two catchments share a similar structure, the methods could usefully be exchanged.
The Suketi inventory of 93 springs and 28 observation wells is the kind of data set that the Gaj
model lacks for validation; a similar inventory in the Gaj would greatly strengthen its
conclusions. In the other direction, the AHP workflow could map potential across the Suketi
hills, where official estimates are not made, and could help choose which of the 53
micro-watersheds should receive recharge structures first. The Suketi chemistry, in turn,
offers a baseline against which future surveys in either catchment can be compared.

\subsection{Towards an Integrated Framework}

Drawing these threads together, Fig.~\ref{fig:framework} proposes a framework that links the
three strands of evidence. Field inventory, hydrochemistry with isotopes, and remote sensing
with climate data form the evidence base. These feed three parallel analyses: resource
estimation and morphometry; quality indices and facies; and multi-criteria mapping of
potential and infiltration. The outputs are then combined into a quality-screened recharge
suitability map, delineated at the scale of individual springsheds, and ranked by demand and
vulnerability. Implementation should involve local institutions and traditional systems, and a
monitoring network of wells and springs should feed results back into the model so that
weights and site choices can be revised over time. Such an approach is consistent with the
basin-scale planning required by national policy \citep{mowr2002} and with the springshed
approach recommended for the Himalaya \citep{niti2018,tambe2012}.

\section{Research Gaps and Future Directions}

The comparison points to the following priorities for future work in the Suketi, Gaj and
similar Himalayan catchments.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textit{Long-term monitoring.} Establish a network of observation wells and gauged
        springs, measured at least seasonally, to provide time series of levels, discharge and
        chemistry and to update the 2004 and 2009 baselines.
  \item \textit{Springshed delineation.} Combine geological mapping with stable isotopes and
        tracer tests to identify the recharge area of important springs before structures are
        built.
  \item \textit{Wider quality coverage.} Add trace elements, microbial indicators and emerging
        contaminants, and extend sampling to the Gaj watershed, with attention to fluoride in
        granite terrain and nitrate in growing towns.
  \item \textit{Robust mapping.} Test the sensitivity of AHP weights, compare AHP with
        statistical or machine-learning methods, and validate maps with independent field data
        rather than with layers derived from the same inputs \citep{machiwal2011}.
  \item \textit{Recharge accounting.} Estimate the volume each proposed structure can add,
        using water-balance or simple numerical models, and then monitor actual performance
        after construction.
  \item \textit{Climate scenarios.} Link groundwater potential to projected changes in rainfall,
        snow and temperature so that structures are designed for future rather than past
        conditions \citep{immerzeel2010}.
  \item \textit{Social and economic feasibility.} Assess land tenure, community willingness,
        maintenance arrangements and costs and benefits, and consider how traditional systems
        can be restored alongside new structures.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusions}

This review compared three studies of groundwater in two Lesser Himalayan catchments of
Himachal Pradesh. Taken together, they show that groundwater in these mountains has two
contrasting parts. Valley-fill aquifers such as the Balh valley hold usable, under-developed
reserves with shallow water levels, while hill tracts depend on small, fracture-controlled
springs whose flow is low and strongly seasonal. The groundwater of the Suketi basin is of good
chemical quality and suitable for drinking and irrigation, but local fluoride in granite and
rising nitrate in towns show that quality cannot be taken for granted. In the Gaj watershed,
geospatial analysis shows a decline in the most favourable recharge zones between 2010 and
2022, together with weaker monsoons, warmer maximum temperatures and a growing population.

The three studies complement one another: the first addresses quantity, the second quality and
the third spatial planning. Their main shortcomings are short records, limited field
validation of maps, missing isotopic and microbial data, and little assessment of recharge
volumes, costs or community roles. Future work should join these strands within a single
framework that screens recharge sites for water quality, works at the scale of spring
catchments, and is tested against a sustained monitoring network. Such an approach would give
planners in the Himalayan foothills a stronger basis for protecting both the quantity and the
quality of groundwater for the long term.
